Why Isn’t Your Mouth Part of Your Body?

An exploration of the strange institutional boundary between medicine and dentistry

← Back to The Bear's Den

There is something slightly absurd about the way we have organised the human body. Your heart belongs to medicine. Your lungs belong to medicine. Your liver belongs to medicine. Your skin belongs to medicine. Your teeth? Well, that depends. There is a different professional. A different clinic. Often a different insurance policy. A different billing system. Different records. A different educational pathway. Sometimes, remarkably, even the same anatomical territory can change categories depending on why you are there. A tooth removed because it is causing trouble might be considered "dental". The same tooth removed because it needs to come out before cancer treatment can become "medical". The mouth is physically attached to the rest of us. Institutionally, however, it has acquired something closer to a border. And once you notice the border, it becomes difficult to stop noticing it.

The body, with a line drawn through it

The obvious response is that dentistry is different. Of course it is. Dentists perform procedures that require highly specialised technical skills. They work in a uniquely constrained anatomical environment. They need to understand occlusion, restorative materials, biomechanics, endodontics, periodontics, prosthodontics, orthodontics and a host of other things that physicians do not routinely practise. Nobody is arguing that every doctor should start doing root canals. But that is not really the question.

The more interesting question is why expertise in the mouth became organised as a largely separate profession in the first place. The modern story of professional dentistry is often traced to Baltimore in 1840, when the Baltimore College of Dental Surgery became the first dental school. Before formal dental education, dental care in the United States had been provided by a mixture of physicians, surgeons, apprentices, barber-dentists and practitioners with varying degrees of formal training. The creation of a dedicated dental school was, in many ways, a progressive development. Dentistry was becoming a scientific profession rather than a craft learnt through apprenticeship. The early curriculum itself did not imagine the mouth as biologically unrelated to the rest of the body. General medicine had an important place alongside technical dental training.

And yet dentistry gradually became an autonomous profession. Paul Allison, professor of dental public health at McGill University, offered an important qualification to the historical story when I spoke to him. The model we have today, he argued, is not necessarily the only model that could have emerged. It is the model that developed in response to the economic and social circumstances of the time. Its existence therefore does not prove that it was inevitable, but neither does its historical contingency mean that specialisation itself was a mistake.

That distinction is useful. The separation was not simply the result of somebody deciding that the mouth was not part of the body. It was messier than that. Questions of cost, equipment, professional identity, technical training and institutional power all played a role. Independent dental schools offered space for specialised technical training and expensive equipment. Professional boundaries emerged. Then those boundaries acquired institutions. Institutions acquired curricula. Curricula acquired licensing requirements. Eventually, something that had once been a historical arrangement began to look like an anatomical fact. We educate people into categories and sometimes later mistake those categories for reality.

What happens when we train people separately?

A medical student learns to think about the body in one institutional environment. A dental student learns to think about it in another. Neither education is necessarily wrong. But the division can create a strange blind spot. There is now substantial interest in oral–medical integration. Importantly, integration does not necessarily mean erasing professional boundaries. It can mean creating better connections between them: interprofessional education, shared referral pathways, collaboration between oral and medical professionals, co-location and the incorporation of oral health into primary care and broader universal-health-coverage approaches.

The problem is therefore not simply that doctors and dentists do not talk to one another. It is structural. Separate education can lead to separate professional cultures. Separate professional cultures can lead to separate records. Separate records can lead to weak referral systems. Separate referral systems are reinforced by separate financing structures. The result is something surprisingly mundane: two healthcare systems dealing with different parts of the same person.

A primary-care doctor may routinely ask about smoking, diet, exercise, sleep and medications while barely looking inside the patient's mouth. A dentist may spend an hour examining that mouth and yet operate within a system where the patient's broader medical history is incomplete, inaccessible or simply not part of the workflow. Neither clinician necessarily provides poor care. The problem may be that two good systems do not always talk to one another.

Should dentists become doctors?

One possible solution is obvious. Why not make dentistry a medical speciality? Medical school first. Dental training afterwards. There is something attractive about the idea. A dentist who has spent years learning general medicine before specialising in the mouth might be more comfortable with chronic disease, medications, mental health, paediatrics, ageing and the social circumstances surrounding illness.

But then another question appears. What exactly would we gain? There are already oral and maxillofacial surgeons, oral medicine specialists, ENT surgeons, head and neck surgeons, physicians and other specialists whose work overlaps with parts of the territory around the mouth, face and neck. If dentistry became another medical speciality, would we genuinely integrate care? Or would we simply create another layer of specialisation?

This question became particularly interesting when I spoke with three healthcare professionals in India: a paediatrician, a physician working in health insurance and a dentist with training in public health. Their perspectives did not converge neatly. And perhaps that is precisely what makes them useful.

A global perspective

The paediatrician I spoke with felt that greater integration should begin much earlier, at the level of professional education. Rather than educating future doctors and dentists almost entirely in separate worlds, they suggested that students could begin with a shared foundation in medicine and health before moving into specialised training. The reasoning was not that dentists need to become physicians. It was that doctors need to understand the mouth. From a paediatric perspective, this is particularly important. The mouth can provide clues about nutrition, development, infection and other aspects of a child's health. Oral findings can sometimes be part of a wider clinical picture rather than an isolated dental problem.

The same principle works in the other direction. A dentist sees patients repeatedly and may notice changes in health, medication use, behaviour or development over time. A dentist who understands enough general medicine to recognise when something falls outside their scope can become an important point of detection and referral.

The physician working in insurance raised a related but different concern. For them, the more immediate boundary was financial. Many oral procedures are still treated as discretionary or elective within insurance systems. Yet there are obvious grey zones. Severe pain is not cosmetic. Infection is not cosmetic. A suspicious oral lesion is not cosmetic. And oral conditions that intersect with cancer, systemic disease or urgent medical treatment cannot easily be categorised as an optional purchase. The insurance perspective raises a deceptively simple question: When does a dental problem become a healthcare problem?

The third perspective was more sceptical of the idea that dentists should simply be trained in much more general medicine. The dentist I interviewed argued that this could become an inefficient use of professional training. Dentists already require extensive specialised education to diagnose and treat oral disease. Requiring them to acquire a large additional body of general medical knowledge might not necessarily improve care. Their proposed solution was different. Rather than making dentists more like doctors, make the system better at connecting dentists and doctors. That means better referral pathways, better recognition of when a problem extends beyond the mouth, better communication between professionals and better public-health policy to ensure that urgent and essential oral healthcare is not excluded simply because it happens to be delivered by a dentist.

Allison's perspective arrives at a similar destination from a different route. He argued that physicians such as paediatricians and geriatricians do not need detailed dental expertise. They need enough oral-health knowledge to recognise that something is wrong and know when to refer to a mouth specialist. Dentists, meanwhile, need enough knowledge of general health conditions, particularly conditions that can manifest orally, to recognise when their patients need attention beyond dentistry. The distinction is important. Integration does not have to mean merger. The question may not be whether dentists should become doctors. It may be whether doctors and dentists should become better at caring for the same person.

Your mouth is also a record

There is another reason the mouth makes such a fascinating starting point. Teeth are not simply pieces of mineral sitting inside us. They can preserve information about development, diet and the conditions surrounding early life. Researchers have studied biological markers in teeth and anthropologists have long used them to investigate aspects of human history. But the idea does not need to depend on exotic examples.

The mouth is one of the places where biology, behaviour, environment and society become visible at the same time. Diet appears there. Smoking appears there. Age appears there. Some medications leave their traces there. Some diseases manifest there. And so does neglect.

This is where the distinction between oral health and "general health" starts to feel increasingly artificial. Not because every disease in the mouth causes disease elsewhere. It does not. And not because every claim about an "oral–systemic connection" is necessarily true.

Allison made a useful distinction here. Rather than chasing causal explanations for every association between gum disease and conditions such as dementia, Alzheimer's disease or depression, he argued that public health should often begin with the common-risk-factor approach. The same social and behavioural conditions can influence multiple diseases without one necessarily causing the other. That is a more useful place for public health to intervene.

The mouth does not exist outside the social determinants of health. And this is where oral health becomes a public-health issue rather than simply a dental one. A person who cannot afford a dentist does not experience their inability to pay as an institutional debate about professional boundaries. They experience pain. A child with untreated dental disease does not experience a philosophical distinction between medical and dental care. They experience difficulty eating, sleeping or concentrating. An older person with missing or painful teeth does not experience the problem as an insurance classification. They experience difficulty chewing.

The WHO's global oral-health strategy frames oral health within universal health coverage and primary healthcare, calling for essential oral healthcare to be incorporated into primary healthcare and broader universal-health-coverage approaches.

This matters because oral disease is not distributed randomly. Access to oral healthcare is shaped by income, geography, age, disability, social marginalisation and other determinants of health. The politics of dental care therefore cannot be separated from the politics of healthcare more broadly.

The politics and economics of a tooth

Carlos Quiñonez's work on the politics of dental care in Canada makes this particularly clear. In his analysis, Quiñonez traces how the organisation, financing and delivery of dental care emerged from historical policy choices, professional interests and competing ideas about individual and social responsibility. His analysis connects the political economy of dentistry to the distribution of oral disease and access to care.

The crucial point is that the dental divide was never purely biological. It was political. It was economic. It was historical. Canada provides a particularly revealing example because its universal public health insurance is not, in practice, universal coverage for every kind of healthcare. Provincial and territorial plans cover medically necessary hospital and physician services, as well as certain surgical-dental services, but routine dental care delivered in community practices has historically sat largely outside that core benefit. Coverage for other dental services varies by province and territory and is often targeted towards particular groups. Access can therefore depend not only on clinical need but also on employment benefits, income, geography and eligibility for a public programme.

This history is not simply a story of professional self-interest. Professional autonomy, the practical organisation of private practice, public budgets and competing ideas about individual and collective responsibility have all shaped the system. As Quiñonez argues, the way dental care is financed and delivered reflects political choices as well as clinical ones. The current arrangement should be understood neither as inevitable nor as the product of one group acting alone.

Institutions have interests. Professions have interests. Governments have interests. Patients have interests. Healthcare systems are what happens when all of these collide.

Consider a person with an infected tooth. The pain and the consequences for eating, sleeping and working are real, but the route to treatment may depend on whether the person has dental benefits, can pay privately, qualifies for a public programme or needs hospital-based care. The clinical problem does not become less serious because its financing sits outside the main public insurance system.

Lisa Watson, a Canadian registered dental hygienist and co-author of work on oral–medical integration, offered another useful perspective. She described how medical and dental insurance have historically developed around different assumptions and payment arrangements. Dental benefits can be organised around routine and recurring care, while public medical insurance is primarily built around insured physician and hospital services. That history helps explain the divide, although it does not settle whether the divide remains desirable.

Canada's newer Canadian Dental Care Plan (CDCP) is an important change to this landscape, but it is not the same as universal, first-dollar dental coverage. It is a federal programme for eligible residents who lack access to private dental benefits and have an adjusted family net income below $90,000. It covers a range of preventive and treatment services, but co-payments and charges above the plan's established fees can still leave patients with costs. Eligibility and coverage rules matter, and access to a benefit does not guarantee that a provider is available nearby.

The scale of the gap helps explain why this matters. The Public Health Agency of Canada's analysis of the 2024 Canadian Oral Health Survey found that nearly one-third of people in Canada were in a dental-coverage gap. People without insurance were much more likely to avoid seeing an oral-health professional because of cost than people with insurance. The CDCP is intended to narrow that gap, but its impact depends on more than enrolment: people also need participating providers, appointments they can reach and a clear understanding of what the plan will pay.

The problem with calling dental care “elective”

There is another cultural distinction hiding underneath the financing debate. We have allowed the word dentistry to cover two very different worlds.

On one side are pain, infection, difficulty eating and the consequences of untreated disease. On the other are services sought primarily for appearance, such as whitening or some cosmetic procedures. The distinction matters, but the fact that both are delivered by dental professionals can blur public understanding of what essential oral healthcare is.

This is where Mary Otto's book, Teeth: The Story of Beauty, Inequality, and the Struggle for Oral Health in America, becomes important. Otto uses the mouth to expose something much larger about class. Her reporting connects unequal access to dental care with poverty, employment, education, social mobility and the way physical appearance becomes a marker of social position.

A healthy, attractive smile can function as social capital. A visibly neglected mouth can become a stigma. That means oral disease is not experienced only through pain. It can affect whether someone feels comfortable speaking, smiling or being seen. It can influence how they are perceived in workplaces and social settings. The consequences of untreated oral disease can therefore extend into precisely those areas of life that health policy often struggles to measure.

This is where oral health becomes a question of human dignity.

The distinction between essential and elective care matters. A procedure sought primarily to change appearance is not the same as treatment for pain, infection or impaired function. Some cases sit in a grey area, and clinical context matters. But the existence of cosmetic dentistry should not make the whole field of oral healthcare seem discretionary.

The word "dental" becomes shorthand for "something you can pay for yourself". And that is where an administrative distinction becomes a social one.

Public health is not only about treatment

Allison's perspective adds another layer here. If health inequalities are produced by social determinants, then treating people one tooth or one disease at a time cannot be the ultimate goal.

Dentists can treat disease. Doctors can treat disease. Hospitals can treat complications. But public health has always been interested in something upstream: why did the disease occur in the first place?

Diet, tobacco, alcohol, income, education, housing, working conditions, commercial environments and access to preventive services can shape health across the body. The same factors can shape the mouth. That means the long-term response cannot simply be to produce more dentists or make dental treatment cheaper. It must also involve changing the environments in which people live.

This is one of the reasons the common-risk-factor approach is so important. If the same determinants contribute to multiple diseases, then interventions that improve those determinants can benefit the mouth and the rest of the body simultaneously.

The dentist is still necessary. The doctor is still necessary. But neither is the endpoint of public health. The endpoint is a healthier population.

What does meaningful integration actually look like?

This may be the most important question. If integration does not necessarily mean merging the professions, what should it look like?

Watson's answer was practical. One possibility is co-located care, where medical and oral-health professionals work within the same community health centre. Shared electronic health records could make it easier for clinicians to understand a patient's wider health context. Cross-disciplinary screening could allow dental professionals to identify signs of broader health problems while medical professionals become more comfortable recognising oral disease.

There is evidence that such models can work, but also that they have limits. For example, research on medical–dental integration in community health centres has examined how integrating oral-health professionals into primary care can expand access to preventive oral-health services, while restorative dental care may remain a challenge.

And a shared health record cannot solve everything. It cannot create a dentist where there is no dentist. It cannot build a clinic in a remote community. It cannot solve transport barriers. It cannot make a referral useful when the receiving service does not exist.

This distinction is important because it is easy to mistake administrative integration for healthcare integration. A shared record is a tool. A referral pathway is a tool. Co-location is a tool. None is a substitute for infrastructure, workforce or financing.

The Canadian Dental Care Plan makes the financing debate more concrete. It is a significant expansion of public support for people who previously lacked dental benefits, and it covers services ranging from examinations and preventive care to fillings, root canals, dentures and oral surgery, subject to the plan's rules. Government figures show that nearly five million people had used the plan to access care at least once by September 2026. That is a substantial change in access, but it is not proof that every barrier has disappeared. Some members still face co-payments or additional charges, and service availability can vary by region. The plan therefore illustrates both the potential and the limits of public financing: it can reduce the price barrier without automatically solving workforce, geographic or administrative barriers.

Public financing can make care more affordable. It cannot, on its own, make care available.

The patient should not have to become the messenger

This may be the most practical definition of integration.

A physician who sees an oral lesion should know when it needs dental or specialist assessment. A dentist seeing a patient with poorly controlled diabetes should understand why that medical context matters. A paediatrician should recognise when dental decay is becoming a health problem rather than simply a dental appointment. A dentist should recognise when a medication, mental-health issue, nutritional problem or other medical condition needs attention beyond their scope.

None of this requires every clinician to know everything. It requires them to know enough. Enough to recognise. Enough to refer. Enough to communicate. Enough to understand that the person sitting in front of them does not become a different patient when they cross the threshold of another clinic.

The same principle applies to the patient record. Medical records talking to dental records can help. But the purpose is not to create an enormous database of everything that has ever happened to someone. It is to ensure that information relevant to the next clinical decision does not disappear at the professional boundary.

And crucially, the patient should not have to become the messenger.

From the womb onwards

Perhaps integration makes most sense when it begins before people become complicated patients. Lisa Watson and Kyla Oshanek's work on oral–medical integration from the prenatal and perinatal period argues that oral health professionals can play a role in prevention and lifelong health from the earliest stages of life.

Pregnancy and early childhood are particularly revealing examples because they already require multiple professionals to work around the same person. An obstetrician cares for the pregnant patient. A midwife may provide additional care. A family physician may be involved. A paediatrician becomes involved after birth. A public-health nurse may support the family. Why should oral health have to wait for a toothache before joining the team?

The same logic can continue throughout the life course. Children can encounter oral health as part of routine health promotion. Primary care can incorporate basic oral screening and referral. Dental professionals can participate more systematically in chronic disease prevention. Community health centres can provide models for shared care.

And when severe dental disease does occur, it should be recognised for what it is. Dental decay is the most common non-communicable disease globally. In the UK, tooth decay is a leading cause of hospital admission among young children, with thousands undergoing extractions each year. Many of these procedures require general anaesthesia because of the severity of disease, the age of the child or the circumstances in which treatment is being delivered. That is not to say that children routinely receive general anaesthesia for dental treatment. They do not. The point is that preventable oral disease can become severe enough to require hospital-level care.

The mouth has entered the hospital system. It simply arrived through a different door.

A global problem with local versions

There is no single institutional model that can simply be exported from one country to another. In Canada, universal public insurance covers core hospital and physician services, while much routine dental care has historically relied on private benefits and out-of-pocket spending; the CDCP is now extending public support to eligible residents without access to other dental coverage. In the United States, dental benefits are also commonly separated from medical insurance, though the details vary by plan and programme. The UK has universal medical coverage but a distinct dental system with its own charges and access pressures. India has a different mix of private provision, public services and uneven access. Across Africa and Asia, arrangements vary widely, shaped by workforce capacity, public financing and competing health priorities.

Yet the underlying questions travel remarkably well. Who is responsible for the mouth? Who pays for it? Who is trained to recognise what it is telling us? Who receives the information? Who makes the referral? And what happens when someone cannot afford the answer?

Allison offered another useful reminder here. Health systems operate within their economic and social circumstances. In resource-constrained settings, governments have to prioritise conditions causing the greatest mortality and morbidity. Oral disease may cause enormous suffering and disability without always producing the mortality that commands the same policy attention as other conditions. That is not an argument for ignoring oral health. It is an argument for understanding the politics of prioritisation.

Public health cannot fund everything at once. But when oral disease is repeatedly treated as somebody else's problem, the people who have the least ability to pay for private care are often the ones who bear the greatest burden.

The WHO's global oral-health strategy is useful precisely because it does not require every country to adopt one institutional model. It emphasises prevention, primary healthcare, workforce innovation, universal health coverage and action on the social and commercial determinants of oral disease.

The goal, therefore, is not necessarily to create one universal model of dentistry. It is to stop oral health from being structurally invisible within health systems.

Maybe the mouth is not the problem

Integration does not have to mean abolishing dentistry or absorbing it into medicine. Dentists need deep specialist expertise; physicians need to recognise oral-health problems; and both need workable ways to communicate and refer. The practical goal is a system in which relevant records, referral pathways and financing arrangements support care across the boundary rather than leaving patients to navigate it alone.

That means being more precise about what we fund and how we organise it. Essential care for pain, infection and function should not be dismissed simply because it is dental, while not every procedure has to be publicly funded in the same way. Nor does integration require every physician to practise dentistry or every dentist to complete medical school. It requires a clear account of clinical need, appropriate professional training and a system that connects expertise.

The challenge is to distinguish these things without losing sight of the whole person.

The perspectives in this story approach the problem from different directions. The paediatrician wants doctors to recognise what the mouth can tell them; the dentist cautions against diluting specialised expertise; Watson highlights the financial and administrative boundaries; Quiñonez situates those boundaries in political history; and Mary Otto shows how unequal access becomes pain, stigma and lost opportunity. Allison's public-health perspective brings the threads together: better recognition and referral matter, but so do prevention, public investment and the social conditions that shape health.

These perspectives do not require one profession to absorb the other. They point towards a more modest, and perhaps more useful, ambition: make the system better at caring for the same person across professional boundaries.

So why isn't your mouth part of your body? It is. Biologically, obviously. Clinically, increasingly. Institutionally, not quite. The question is not whether every boundary should disappear, but whether the boundaries we inherited still serve the people they are meant to care for.

We tend to think that the structures surrounding us emerged because they represent the most rational way of organising reality. Often they emerged from history, professional identity, economics, technology, insurance and regulation. Over time, arrangements that were once choices can begin to look inevitable.

The mouth may simply be a particularly visible example. Specialisation can be extraordinarily useful; fragmented systems of care can be extraordinarily frustrating. The answer may not be to put everything back together, but to remember that the things we separated were never biologically separate.

Once you start looking at the boundary between dentistry and medicine, you begin to wonder where else in healthcare we have mistaken an institutional boundary for a biological one. That is a much bigger question.
